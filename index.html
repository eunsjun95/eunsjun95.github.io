Why single molecules

A transcription factor (P) binding its site on a nucleosome is a reversible reaction, P + L ⇌ PL. Binding happens at rate kon (M−1s−1) and unbinding at rate koff (s−1). I want both numbers, because they answer different questions: how easily the factor finds its site, and how long it stays once it gets there.

Ensemble titration

A bulk measurement averages millions of molecules and reports one relaxation rate:

kobs = kon·[P] + koff

Only the sum is observed. Separating the two terms means fitting a line across many concentrations, and koff comes from an extrapolated intercept. Any differences between individual molecules are averaged away.

Single-molecule dwell times

With TIRF microscopy and FRET, I watch each nucleosome switch between states and time how long it stays in each one:

1/τhigh FRET = kon·[P]
1/τlow FRET = koff

A high-FRET (unbound) dwell can end only by binding, so its rate grows with [P]. A low-FRET (bound) dwell can end only by unbinding, so its rate is koff, the same at every concentration. Each rate is measured directly.

Having kon and koff separately lets me ask sharper questions about my systems:

  • Does a histone modification or HAT activity help a transcription factor get in (faster kon), or help it stay (slower koff)?
  • How does EWS::FLI change the rates at which nucleosomal DNA unwraps and rewraps?
  • Do all molecules behave alike, or are there hidden subpopulations that a bulk average would blur together?
